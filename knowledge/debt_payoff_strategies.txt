THE COST OF CARRYING DEBT

[CFPB-CC] A credit card's interest rate is the price you pay for borrowing
money. For credit cards, interest rates are typically stated as a yearly
rate called the annual percentage rate (APR).

[CFPB-CC] For credit cards specifically, interest is calculated using a
daily periodic rate. Unpaid interest is added to the balance each day, and
the next day's interest is calculated on that larger balance. This daily
compounding applies to credit card debt; other debt types (such as
mortgages or auto loans) may use different compounding schedules.

[CFPB-EF] Relying on credit cards or loans to cover expenses can lead to
debt that is generally harder to pay off.

[CFPB-EF] If a credit card or loan is used to pay for an expense, the
original cost may grow significantly larger because of interest and fees.

The implication for any interest-bearing debt is that the longer it remains
unpaid, the greater the total amount repaid. This is a mathematical
consequence of compound growth, not a claim drawn from the confirmed sources
above. The magnitude depends on the interest rate, compounding schedule,
and payment amount.


TWO COMMON PAYOFF STRATEGIES

NOTE: The following descriptions of the avalanche and snowball methods are
standard definitions used in personal finance education. They are attributed
to the FTC article cited above, but that article's body content was not
independently verified in this environment. These descriptions represent
the standard formulation of each method.

[FTC -- not body-verified] THE AVALANCHE METHOD
Focus extra payments on the debt with the highest interest rate first, while
making minimum payments on all other debts. Once the highest-rate debt is
paid off, direct the freed-up payment toward the next highest-rate debt.

Effect: Under the following assumptions, the avalanche method minimises
the total interest paid over the life of all debts compared to any other
payment-ordering strategy:
  - The same total monthly payment budget is used across all compared
    strategies.
  - Minimum payments are maintained on every debt in every month.
  - When a debt is fully cleared, its freed-up payment amount is
    redirected in full to the next target debt.
  - Interest rates on all debts remain constant throughout.
  - No new debts are added, no debts are refinanced, and no unusual
    fees or penalties are incurred.

Note: minimum required payments may themselves decrease as individual
balances fall (since minimums are often a percentage of the balance).
This assumption list does not require minimums to be fixed dollar amounts.
Real-world outcomes depend on whether these assumptions hold.

[FTC -- not body-verified] THE SNOWBALL METHOD
Focus extra payments on the debt with the smallest outstanding balance first,
while making minimum payments on all other debts. Once the smallest debt is
paid off, direct the freed-up payment toward the next smallest balance.

Effect: The snowball method pays off individual debts faster, which can
provide psychological motivation through visible progress. It may result in
more total interest paid than the avalanche method if the smallest-balance
debts do not also carry the highest interest rates.


CHOOSING BETWEEN THE TWO METHODS

Neither method is universally superior. The choice depends on personal
circumstances:

- If minimising total interest cost is the priority, the avalanche method
  is preferable.
- If staying motivated by seeing debts eliminated quickly matters more,
  the snowball method may be more effective in practice.
- In cases where the smallest-balance debt also carries the highest interest
  rate, both methods produce the same result.


PAYING OFF DEBT VERSUS INVESTING

[FINPATH] FinPath AI includes a debt_vs_invest tool that models two
scenarios over a chosen time horizon: (1) paying off a debt first before
investing, and (2) investing immediately while making only minimum debt
payments. Both scenarios use the same total monthly budget and horizon.

[FINPATH] The tool compares projected net worth (investments minus remaining
debt) at the end of the horizon under each strategy. The comparison is
driven by the relationship between the annual debt interest rate and the
assumed annual investment return rate.

[FINPATH] The agent flags the comparison explicitly: if the debt rate
exceeds the assumed investment rate (default 7% per year), the agent
highlights the debt-payoff-first scenario as producing a higher projected
net worth in the tool's model.

NOTE: The debt_vs_invest tool produces illustrative projections based
entirely on user-supplied figures and stated assumptions. It does not
account for tax treatment, employer matching contributions, risk tolerance,
liquidity needs, or other personal factors. The output is not financial
advice. Actual outcomes will differ from projections.


KEY TAKEAWAYS

- For credit card debt, interest compounds daily; unpaid balances grow
  faster than simple interest calculations would suggest. [CFPB-CC -- confirmed]
- The avalanche method (highest rate first) minimises total interest paid
  under fixed-payment, constant-rate assumptions. [FTC -- not body-verified]
- The snowball method (smallest balance first) reduces the number of open
  debts quickly, which can help motivation. [FTC -- not body-verified]
- When a debt rate exceeds the assumed investment return in the model,
  the debt-payoff-first scenario produces a higher projected net worth.
  This is an illustrative comparison, not financial advice.
- FinPath AI's debt_vs_invest and multi_debt_plan tools model these
  strategies using user-supplied figures as illustrative projections.
